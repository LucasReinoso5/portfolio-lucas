AirTS-Forecast

An end-to-end study comparing statistical forecasting and recurrent neural networks across five major air pollutants in Toulouse.

Context
ENIT — Two-person supervised project
Timeline
February–June 2026
Data
21 Mar 2021–21 Mar 2026

01 — The challenge

Forecast complex urban pollution dynamics

Air pollution is shaped by seasonal cycles, meteorological conditions, and irregular human activity. The project investigated how classical time-series models and deep learning could forecast pollutant concentrations and support future decision-making applications.

The study covered NO₂, NOx, O₃, PM10, and PM2.5 using five years of measurements from Toulouse.

02 — My role

Collaboration across the full workflow

Developed as a two-person engineering project with Tiago Toloczko Ross under the supervision of Dr. Raymond Houé Ngouna. Both team members contributed throughout the complete lifecycle rather than dividing the project into isolated ownership areas.

I worked across data acquisition and preparation, exploratory analysis, statistical forecasting, recurrent neural-network experiments, hyperparameter optimization, model evaluation, and technical reporting.

03 — Approach

One framework, multiple forecasting strategies

  • Combined pollutant measurements from GEOD’AIR with meteorological variables from ERA5-Land.
  • Studied trends, stationarity, seasonality, periodicity, and temporal memory using ADF, PSD, moving averages, STL, ACF, and PACF.
  • Established statistical baselines with ARIMA, SARIMA, and Holt-Winters.
  • Compared RNN, LSTM, Bi-LSTM, GRU, and hybrid RNN-LSTM architectures.
  • Optimized model depth, hidden dimensions, batch size, look-back window, forecast horizon, and learning rate with Optuna.
  • Tested both univariate and multivariate configurations using pollutant-specific environmental features.

04 — Evaluation

Performance depends on the pollutant

Models were compared using MAPE, RMSE, MAE, and R². Recurrent architectures captured nonlinear variability and high-frequency events more effectively than the smoother classical baselines, but accuracy differed substantially across pollutants.

More variables did not automatically mean better forecasts.

Meteorological inputs improved some LSTM and GRU configurations, while more complex architectures sometimes degraded. The effect depended on both the target pollutant and model architecture.

06 — What I learned

Evaluate the system, not only the score

The project reinforced that forecast quality cannot be understood through a single aggregate metric. Architecture complexity, temporal shifts, pollutant chemistry, training data, and computational cost all affect whether a model is genuinely useful.

It also provided practical experience managing a complete engineering study in an international team, from initial scope and iterative experimentation to final documentation.
